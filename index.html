🚀 Coolest Projects — Our Driving Goal

Coolest Projects runs as a parallel strand across KS3: alongside the taught units, regular time is devoted to learning and developing an original digital creation. Students don't only apply skills from the units — they also learn the new things their project needs, building genuine ownership, independence, and pride in their work.

🌐 Coolest Projects Online 2026: cp-online-2026  •  📋 Categories: categories  •  ℹ️ About: about-us

Each creation fits an official category:

  • 🧩 Scratch
  • 📱 Mobile Apps
  • 🌍 Web
  • 🎮 Games
  • 🔌 Hardware
  • 💻 Advanced Programming
  • 🤖 Artificial Intelligence

A flexible, multi-year journey. Projects need not be finished in a single year — an ambitious creation can be started in one year and continued and completed in a later one, so students can pursue genuinely substantial builds over time. The work culminates in a showcase — primarily an in-school celebration event where students present their creations to peers, staff, and families. Entering Coolest Projects Online is an optional, additional route for those who want to share their work globally.

Milestones across the year

HT1 · Autumn — Discover & ProposeExplore the categories; start a new idea or continue a previous project; agree a short plan.
HT2–HT4 · Autumn–Spring — Learn & DevelopDedicated time each half-term to learn the skills the project needs and build it, with progress reviews and peer feedback.
HT5 · Summer — FinaliseTest, polish, and prepare to present (and, optionally, enter Coolest Projects Online).
HT6 · Summer — Showcase & ReflectPresent at an in-school showcase event; celebrate and reflect against the rubric. Ambitious projects may carry on into the next year.

Assessment: the project is the flagship AO4 — Creativity, Design & Project Work strand (with AO1 Programming where it involves code). Formative — the plan, progress reviews, and peer feedback. Summative — each year, the project's progress and showcase presentation are judged against the Emerging → Developing → Secure → Mastery rubric (a multi-year project is credited for that year's development, not only its final completion).

E-Safety and Digital Responsibility Across KS3

E-safety is explicitly taught at the start of each year (within the Year 7, 8 and 9 HT1 units) and embedded across all areas of study as part of a holistic approach to responsible computing. Throughout every topic, students are consistently reminded to use technology safely, ethically, and responsibly—whether they are communicating online, programming, managing data, creating digital content, controlling physical devices, or engaging with emerging technologies such as Artificial Intelligence.

Several strands spiral through all three years, each deepening from Year 7 to Year 9 so that skills are continually revisited, reinforced, and extended.

🗂️ Microsoft 365 — Core Productivity Skills

Every year begins with a Microsoft 365 unit focused on progressively mastering the core productivity apps — students learn and practise Word, Excel, PowerPoint, OneDrive, Teams and email:

  • Year 7 (Part I) · Getting started: Save/organise in OneDrive; use Teams and email; simple Word documents and PowerPoint presentations; first steps in Excel (entering data, a chart).
  • Year 8 (Part II) · Building fluency: Collaborate in OneDrive; professional email; structured Word documents (formatting, tables, images); effective PowerPoint design; Excel data analysis (formulas, charts, sort/filter).
  • Year 9 (Part III) · Independent & efficient: Polished, audience-appropriate work across the apps, and advanced Excel (functions, pivot tables, dashboards) for real data analysis.

🖥️ Computer Science — Computational Thinking & Theory

The HT2 unit each year develops core Computer Science — computational thinking and the theory of how computers work — bridging toward GCSE, brought to life with hands-on apps, tools and simulators (see Interactive Tools & Simulators below):

  • Year 7 — Algorithms, Logic & Computer Basics: algorithms, flowcharts & pseudo-code, and the main parts of a computer (CPU, memory, I/O).
  • Year 8 — Number Systems & Logic: binary and number systems; logic gates and truth tables.
  • Year 9 — Computer Systems, Networks & Data: how computers work (CPU & fetch–execute cycle), how networks and the internet work, and data representation (text, images, sound).

Hands-on low-level programming and building logic circuits run as extension threads.

🧩 Scratch — Block-Based Programming

Block-based programming with Scratch gives a consistent, low-barrier route into computational thinking that deepens each year before students move to text-based code:

  • Year 7 — Foundations: Sequence, variables, loops, and conditionals to control sprites, motion, and interactivity.
  • Year 8 — Procedures & Procedural Graphics: Custom blocks (procedures), randomness, and the pen tool; introducing abstraction and decomposition.
  • Year 9 — Animations, Games & Simulations: Multiple sprites, cloning, broadcasting/events, and custom scripts for richer games and simulations — a bridge toward Python.

🤖 Physical Computing with the BBC Micro:bit

Physical computing runs as a progressive strand so students repeatedly connect code to the physical world. Students work with physical BBC Micro:bit devices — plugging them into the computer to download (flash) and run their own programs — alongside the on-screen simulator for quick testing:

  • Year 7 — Foundations: LEDs, buttons, and simple inputs/outputs in block-based MakeCode.
  • Year 8 — Sensors & Data: On-board sensors (temperature, light, accelerometer, compass) to capture, process, and respond to real-world data.
  • Year 9 — Communication, IoT & Python: Radio communication and networked/IoT projects in MakeCode, then a first taste of text-based Python (MicroPython) — bridging toward GCSE (with fuller Python as an extension).

⛏️ Minecraft Education — Creative Coding & Collaboration

Using Minecraft Education, students build, code, and collaborate in an engaging shared world. It provides a collaborative problem-solving environment where students apply computational thinking, automation, decomposition, and project management in meaningful, motivating contexts, while practising digital citizenship through respectful teamwork. The built-in Code Builder provides block-based and (later) text-based coding, and collaboration runs through every stage:

  • Year 7 — Explore & Build: Navigate and build collaboratively, practise digital citizenship, and take first steps with Code Builder (the Agent and simple block commands).
  • Year 8 — Build & Code: Use loops, conditionals and coordinates in Code Builder to automate builds, through cross-curricular STEAM challenges.
  • Year 9 — Code & Create: Code with MakeCode and a taste of Python, on larger collaborative problem-solving builds linked to CS concepts (and optional AI lessons).

🧠 Artificial Intelligence

As Artificial Intelligence rapidly reshapes how people live, learn, and work, students need to understand how it works, use it responsibly, and think critically about its impact on society. This AI-literacy strand builds progressively:

  • Year 7 — AI Foundations: What AI is, everyday examples of AI, using AI tools responsibly, and a first hands-on taste (e.g. Google's Teachable Machine or an Hour of AI activity). (HT6)
  • Year 8 — AI Literacy: A responsible-AI-use refresher in HT1 (recognising AI-generated content; checking accuracy and bias), then studying what AI is and how it works, its uses, benefits, and limitations; research and present on its impact on society (HT6).
  • Year 9 — Experience AI: Hands-on machine learning, interactive AI systems, and examining bias, transparency, and ethics.

🛠️ Interactive Tools & Simulators

Custom-built tools, web apps and simulators make abstract concepts concrete, letting students explore, experiment, build — and even act concepts out. This is an evolving toolkit — more will be added as the course develops:

  • The Digits Explorer — representing numbers with symbols and investigating number systems. (Year 8 — Number Systems & Logic.)
  • The Logic Trainer Simulator — learning and practising logic gates and simple circuits (and their truth tables). (Year 8 — Number Systems & Logic.)
  • The School Computer Simulator (TheSchoolComputer.xlsx) — a computer modelled as a school: the office = CPU, Primary Rooms = instructions, secondary rooms = data. Students can write and run simple programs in the spreadsheet, and can also run it unplugged — using real school locations and moving around to read and execute instructions as the computer's parts. (Used in the Computer Science (HT2) units, Years 7–9 — writing programs is an extension.)

📊 Research & Presentation Projects

Students regularly explore, research, and search for information and communicate findings through presentations and infographics, using Microsoft apps (PowerPoint, Word, Excel) and tools such as Canva. Recurring themes:

  • 📊 Data Collection & Analysis — gathering, analysing, and visualising data, then presenting insights responsibly.
  • 🛡️ E-Safety & Digital Wellbeing — researching and promoting safe, ethical, positive use of technology.
  • 🤖 Artificial Intelligence — how AI works, its uses, benefits, limitations, and ethical impact on society.

Assessment is continuous and supportive, combining frequent low-stakes Formative Assessment (FA) with an end-of-unit Summative Assessment (SA) — mirroring the FA/SA checkpoints in the scheme of work.

  • Formative (FA): ongoing checks — questioning, live demos, peer/self-review, debugging challenges, short quizzes, and feedback on work-in-progress — used to adapt teaching and help students improve before the summative point.
  • Summative (SA): an end-of-unit judgement — usually the unit project plus a short knowledge check — reported against the shared rubric below.

Every unit lists its learning objectives, and achievement is reported in four bands: Emerging → Developing → Secure → Mastery. Each unit is tagged with the Assessment Objective(s) it primarily develops, shown with a short plain-English label (e.g. AO1 Programming).

Some unit content is marked Extension — deeper, more challenging work that stretches confident and high-attaining students, especially those who finish the core early or need greater demand. Extensions also provide enrichment to draw on when time allows. Everything else forms the core entitlement that all students cover.

In Year 7, students build a strong foundation in digital skills and begin developing computational thinking. The year focuses on becoming confident, safe, and creative users of technology, while starting to think like computer scientists.

HT1: Microsoft 365 (Part I) – Getting Started

Topics covered: After a start on E-Safety and digital wellbeing (online safety, privacy, online identity), the unit focuses on learning and practising the core Microsoft 365 apps: saving and organising work in OneDrive, using Teams and email, creating and formatting documents in Word, building presentations in PowerPoint, and taking first steps with data in Excel (entering data and making a simple chart). Students also get a brief introduction to using everyday AI responsibly — revisited in more depth later.

Project: Students create a presentation or infographic — e.g. "Pros and Cons of Living in a Digital World" — promoting safe, positive technology use, including some simple data they have gathered and charted in Excel.

Extension: Go further in Excel — use functions like SUM and AVERAGE, sort and filter data, or add conditional formatting to highlight key results.

Objectives: Students can save and organise files in OneDrive, use Teams and email, create and format a document in Word, build a presentation in PowerPoint, enter and chart simple data in Excel, explain key e-safety principles, and use AI tools responsibly.

Assessment: Formative — login/file and spreadsheet tasks, an e-safety & AI-awareness quiz, draft feedback. Summative — a data-informed presentation/infographic + a brief e-safety/AI check. (AO2 Digital Literacy · AO3 Systems & Data · AO5 Citizenship & E-Safety)

HT2: Algorithms, Logic & Computer Basics

Topics covered: Students explore what algorithms are and how they describe everyday processes, learning to represent them using flowcharts and pseudo-code. They are then introduced to Turing Tumble — a marble-powered mechanical computer, used as both a physical device and an online simulator — learning how the machine works (board, blue/red marbles, gravity, release levers) and using ramps to build simple paths that route the marbles, making sequence and logic hands-on. They also meet The School Computer Simulator (TheSchoolComputer.xlsx) and learn the main parts of a computer (CPU, memory, I/O). 🔗 Turing Tumble · Simulator (ttsim)

Project: Students design, build and test a simple Turing Tumble path (on the physical device and/or simulator) that routes marbles to a chosen outcome, representing their solution as an algorithm (flowchart/pseudo-code) and debugging it.

Extension: Use a small set of simple instructions in The School Computer to write and run a first low-level program (on screen or as an unplugged "human computer" activity).

Objectives: Students can explain what an algorithm is, represent it as a flowchart and pseudo-code, name the main parts of a computer (CPU, memory, I/O), and build and test a simple Turing Tumble path.

Assessment: Formative — unplugged tasks, flowchart starters, Turing Tumble path challenges, peer-checking. Summative — a working Turing Tumble path, with its algorithm. (AO1 Programming · AO3 Systems & Data)

HT3: Scratch (Part I) – Introduction to Programming

Topics covered: Students are introduced to programming through Scratch, using sequence, variables, loops, and conditionals to control sprites, motion, and behaviour, and to add simple interactivity.

Project: Design an interactive game or animation, demonstrating an understanding of program structure and debugging.

Extension: Add a score, a timer, or a second level to your game, or a start screen with instructions.

Objectives: Students can use sequence, variables, loops and conditionals, and build and debug an interactive program.

Assessment: Formative — guided builds and debugging challenges. Summative — an interactive game or animation. (AO1 Programming)

HT4: Micro:bit (Part I) – Introduction to Physical Computing

Topics covered: First steps into physical computing with the BBC Micro:bit in block-based MakeCode — controlling hardware through the LED display, A and B buttons, and simple inputs/outputs, downloading (flashing) programs onto a physical Micro:bit, and using loops and conditionals on a real device.

Project: A short STEAM challenge (e.g. name badge, dice, step counter, or reaction-timer game) — plan, build, test, and debug a working device.

Extension: Add sound or music, use the shake/tilt sensor (accelerometer), or combine two inputs to trigger different outputs.

Objectives: Students can program the Micro:bit with inputs/outputs, loops and conditionals, and test and debug on a real device.

Assessment: Formative — incremental build-and-test tasks. Summative — a STEAM device challenge. (AO1 Programming)

HT5: Minecraft Education (Part I) – Explore & Build

Topics covered: Students are introduced to Minecraft Education, learning to navigate, build, and collaborate responsibly in a shared world. They practise digital citizenship and take first steps with Code Builder, using the Agent and simple block commands to build automatically.

Project: A collaborative themed build (e.g. a sustainable village or eco-world) using basic Agent automation, reflecting on teamwork and responsible online behaviour.

Extension: Use the Agent in a loop to build a larger structure automatically (e.g. a wall or tower), or add a simple redstone mechanism.

Objectives: Students can navigate and build in Minecraft Education, collaborate responsibly, and use Code Builder to give the Agent simple commands.

Assessment: Formative — guided build-and-code tasks and collaboration checks. Summative — a collaborative build with simple Agent code. (AO4 Creativity & Projects · AO1 Programming)

HT6: AI Foundations – Introducing Artificial Intelligence

Topics covered: Students explore the foundations of Artificial Intelligence: what AI is, everyday examples of AI in the technology they use, how they already interact with AI, and how to use AI tools safely, responsibly and ethically. They get a first hands-on taste — e.g. exploring Google's Teachable Machine at a basic level, or an Hour of AI activity — seeing how a computer can be "trained" to recognise things.

Project: A short explainer (presentation or infographic) — "What is AI and how do we use it responsibly?" — or a simple Teachable Machine demo, reflecting on AI's everyday uses and benefits.

Extension: Train a Teachable Machine model with more categories and test where it gets confused — discussing why it makes mistakes.

Objectives: Students can explain what AI is, give everyday examples, describe how to use AI tools responsibly, and try a simple hands-on AI activity.

Assessment: Formative — discussion, everyday-AI examples, a short quiz, exploring an AI tool. Summative — an AI explainer or a simple Teachable Machine demo. (AO5 Citizenship & E-Safety · AO3 Systems & Data)

In Year 8, students think more critically about how computers work and how data is represented, processed, and communicated. They advance their programming and data-handling skills, engage in creative projects, and are introduced to how Artificial Intelligence works.

HT1: Microsoft 365 (Part II) – Building Fluency

Topics covered: After E-Safety (data protection, secure communication, ethical data use), students build fluency with the Microsoft 365 apps: collaborating confidently on files in OneDrive, using email with professional etiquette, producing well-structured documents in Word (formatting, tables, images), designing effective PowerPoint presentations, and carrying out data analysis in Excel (formulas, charts, sorting and filtering), with findings presented in PowerPoint or Canva. They also build their AI awareness — recognising AI-generated content and using AI tools responsibly (revisited in depth in the HT6 AI unit).

Project: A data-driven presentation integrating ICT skills with analytical thinking and responsible data management.

Extension: Go further in Excel — advanced formulas (e.g. IF, VLOOKUP), pivot tables, or an interactive dashboard — to analyse a larger dataset.

Objectives: Students can collaborate on files in OneDrive, use professional email, produce a well-structured Word document, design an effective PowerPoint presentation, analyse and present data in Excel (formulas, charts), and use AI tools responsibly.

Assessment: Formative — data tasks, email-etiquette check, an AI-awareness quiz, draft feedback. Summative — a data-driven presentation. (AO2 Digital Literacy · AO3 Systems & Data · AO5 Citizenship & E-Safety)

HT2: Number Systems & Logic

Topics covered: The mathematical foundations of computing. Students learn about binary and symbolic number systems using The Digits Explorer, then apply this on Turing Tumble (physical device and simulator), using bits to count in binary and release colour patterns (e.g. alternating blue-red-blue-red…). They also learn and practise how logic gates and simple circuits work, building and testing truth tables in The Logic Trainer Simulator. Returning to The School Computer, they meet a larger set of instructions and see how longer low-level programs are built and traced as the CPU fetches and executes each step. 🔗 Turing Tumble · Simulator (ttsim)

Project: Students program Turing Tumble (on the physical device and/or simulator) to count and release a target colour pattern (e.g. alternating blue-red-blue-red…).

Extension: Write a longer program on The School Computer using a wider instruction set, or design an original symbolic number system in The Digits Explorer.

Objectives: Students can convert between number systems, use Turing Tumble bits to count in binary and create colour patterns, and explain and build logic gates and simple circuits (with truth tables).

Assessment: Formative — Digits Explorer, Logic Trainer, Turing Tumble, and School Computer instruction tasks and quizzes. Summative — a Turing Tumble counting/pattern program, plus a short test on number systems and logic gates (extension: a longer School Computer program or an original symbolic number system). (AO3 Systems & Data)

HT3: Scratch (Part II) – Procedures & Procedural Graphics

Topics covered: Building on Year 7, students extend Scratch with custom blocks (procedures), randomness, and the pen tool for procedural graphics, beginning to apply abstraction and decomposition.

Project: Students create a procedural art piece, pattern generator, or simple simulation, demonstrating reusable code.

Extension: Use a custom block with inputs (parameters), or combine the pen tool with randomness so the pattern is different every run.

Objectives: Students can use custom blocks (procedures), randomness and the pen tool, and apply abstraction and decomposition.

Assessment: Formative — procedural-graphics build tasks. Summative — a procedural art piece, pattern generator or simple simulation. (AO1 Programming)

HT4: Micro:bit (Part II) – Sensors & Data

Topics covered: Deepening physical computing with the BBC Micro:bit in MakeCode, exploring the on-board sensors (temperature, light, accelerometer, compass) and learning to capture, process, and respond to real-world data.

Project: A STEAM data project — a prototype that senses and responds to the physical world (e.g. environment monitor, motion alarm, smart wearable), considering safety and sustainability.

Extension: Log sensor readings over time (data logging) and review the results, or combine two sensors for a smarter response.

Objectives: Students can use on-board sensors to capture and respond to real-world data, and build a working sensing prototype.

Assessment: Formative — sensor experiments and incremental builds. Summative — a STEAM sensing project. (AO1 Programming · AO3 Systems & Data)

HT5: Minecraft Education (Part II) – Build & Code

Topics covered: Building on Year 7, students use Code Builder with loops, conditionals and coordinates to automate larger builds, through a cross-curricular STEAM challenge (e.g. recreating a historical site or modelling a science concept), while discussing intellectual property, copyright, and responsible sharing.

Project: A coded build or simulation that meets a cross-curricular brief, demonstrating automation and problem-solving.

Extension: Write a reusable Code Builder function, or use coordinates and loops to build a large patterned structure.

Objectives: Students can use loops, conditionals and coordinates in Code Builder, and apply awareness of intellectual property and copyright when sharing.

Assessment: Formative — coding challenges and peer feedback. Summative — a coded STEAM build. (AO1 Programming · AO4 Creativity & Projects · AO5 Citizenship & E-Safety)

HT6: AI Literacy – How AI Works

Topics covered: The foundations of Artificial Intelligence: what AI is, everyday examples, and how AI systems work, including their benefits, limitations, and common errors, with ethical and responsible use central throughout.

Project: Students research and present on an aspect of AI — how it works, where it is used, and its impact on society — as a presentation or infographic (PowerPoint or Canva).

Extension: Investigate a real AI system (e.g. a recommender or chatbot) — how it works, and its benefits and risks/bias — and present your findings.

Objectives: Students can explain what AI is and how it works, describe its uses, benefits and limitations, and discuss ethics.

Assessment: Formative — discussion, everyday-AI examples, a short quiz. Summative — an AI research presentation/infographic. (AO5 Citizenship & E-Safety · AO3 Systems & Data)

Year 9 prepares students for GCSE-level computing and beyond. They develop fluency in text-based programming, deepen their understanding of computer systems, and explore emerging technologies such as AI and machine learning, with computational thinking and ethical responsibility throughout.

HT1: Microsoft 365 (Part III) – Independent & Efficient

Topics covered: After E-Safety (digital footprints, cybersecurity, responsible computing), students use the Microsoft 365 apps independently and efficiently to produce polished, audience-appropriate work — confident Word, PowerPoint, OneDrive and email — and develop advanced data analysis in Excel (functions, pivot tables and charts) for a real data investigation. They continue to use AI tools responsibly, checking outputs for accuracy and bias.

Project: Students carry out a data investigation — gathering or using a dataset, analysing it in Excel, and presenting clear, evidence-based findings as a presentation or infographic.

Extension: Build an interactive dashboard, model a larger dataset, or clean and validate messy data for more reliable analysis.

Objectives: Students can use the Microsoft 365 apps independently to produce polished work, carry out advanced data analysis in Excel (functions, pivot tables, charts), apply cyber-safety principles, and use AI tools responsibly.

Assessment: Formative — data-analysis tasks and a cyber-safety/AI quiz. Summative — a data investigation presented as a clear, evidence-based report or infographic. (AO2 Digital Literacy · AO3 Systems & Data · AO5 Citizenship & E-Safety)

HT2: Computer Science – Computer Systems, Networks & Data

Topics covered: An explicit Computer Science unit bridging toward GCSE Computer Science, pulling together how computers and the internet work. Students explore computer systems — hardware and software, the CPU, and how it fetches and executes instructions — seeing the fetch–execute cycle in action with The School Computer Simulator (TheSchoolComputer.xlsx; office = CPU, Primary Rooms = instructions, secondary rooms = data), which they can also run as an unplugged "human computer" activity around school. They study how networks and the internet work — what a network is, the internet vs the World Wide Web, how data travels (IP addresses, packets, client–server), key protocols (HTTP, DNS), how web pages are requested and served, and network security. Finally they explore data representation: how binary represents text (ASCII), images (pixels) and sound (samples).

Project: Students produce a "How it works" explainer (presentation or infographic, in PowerPoint or Canva) on a chosen topic — e.g. how the CPU runs a program, how a web page travels from server to screen, or how an image is stored in binary.

Extension: Go hands-on or deeper — write and run a School Computer program using conditional jumps (decisions and loops); build AND/OR logic in Turing Tumble (gears, gear bits, crossovers); or explore hexadecimal, file sizes & compression, or encryption. 🔗 Turing Tumble · Simulator (ttsim)

Objectives: Students can explain how computers work (hardware/software, the CPU and the fetch–execute cycle), how networks and the internet work (protocols and security), and how text, images and sound are represented in binary.

Assessment: Formative — computer-systems, networks and data-representation tasks with short quizzes. Summative — a "How it works" explainer plus a short knowledge test on computer systems, networks and data representation. (AO3 Systems & Data)

HT3: Scratch (Part III) – Animations, Games & Simulations

Topics covered: The final stage of the Scratch strand — students build their most ambitious projects using multiple sprites, layers, cloning, broadcasting/events, and custom scripts to create polished interactive games and simulations, consolidating abstraction and decomposition.

Project: An interactive game or educational simulation / teaching tool that tells a story or solves a problem.

Extension: Add cloning for many objects, a high-score list (lists), or adjustable difficulty to your game or simulation.

Objectives: Students can build a complex game or simulation using sprites, cloning, events and custom scripts, and apply abstraction.

Assessment: Formative — feature-by-feature build tasks. Summative — an interactive game or educational simulation. (AO1 Programming · AO4 Creativity & Projects)

HT4: Micro:bit (Part III) – Communication, IoT & Python

Topics covered: The most advanced stage of the physical-computing strand — using the BBC Micro:bit in MakeCode to explore wireless radio communication between devices and build simple networked / Internet-of-Things (IoT) projects — the main focus of the unit. They then get a first taste of text-based programming, re-using the same device with MicroPython: writing a few lines of Python to make the Micro:bit do something simple and seeing how familiar ideas look in text rather than blocks — lowering the barrier to written syntax and bridging toward GCSE.

Project: Teams build a multi-device system in MakeCode (e.g. a messaging network, remote sensor link, or step-counter leaderboard), applying data communication concepts and discussing the security and privacy of connected devices.

Extension: Make the full blocks → text leap in Python (MicroPython) — write and debug your own program (variables, I/O, loops, conditionals), then go further with functions, lists, or combining sensors with radio. (Or build a larger 3+ Micro:bit network.)

Objectives: Students can use radio to communicate between devices, build a networked/IoT prototype, and write and run a few lines of Python on the Micro:bit, recognising variables, loops and conditionals in text.

Assessment: Formative — radio/networking tasks and short Python read-and-tweak exercises. Summative — a multi-device system (with a few lines of Python as a first taste). (AO1 Programming · AO3 Systems & Data · AO5 Citizenship & E-Safety)

HT5: Minecraft Education (Part III) – Code & Create

Topics covered: In the final stage of the strand, students code in Minecraft with MakeCode and apply their Python start (from the Micro:bit unit) in Code Builder, tackling larger collaborative problem-solving builds linked to Computer Science concepts (algorithms, automation, decomposition). They may also explore AI lessons within Minecraft Education.

Project: A coded world or solution that meets a self-chosen brief — a creative capstone combining building, coding and collaboration.

Extension: Solve the brief using Python in Code Builder, or build an automated / interactive system (e.g. a sensor-triggered build or mini-game).

Objectives: Students can solve problems by coding in Minecraft (blocks and a taste of Python), and collaborate on a complex build.

Assessment: Formative — coding tasks and milestone reviews. Summative — a coded Minecraft project. (AO1 Programming · AO4 Creativity & Projects)

HT6: Experience AI – Foundations and Innovation

Topics covered: A hands-on, two-part exploration of AI. Experience AI (I): machine learning, large language models (LLMs), and AI ethics, training simple models with Google's Teachable Machine. Experience AI (II): creating interactive AI systems (e.g. "Dancing with AI"), introducing human-computer interaction.

Project: Students research and present on AI — its uses, benefits, limitations, and issues of bias, transparency, and responsible use — as a presentation or infographic (PowerPoint or Canva), alongside their hands-on AI build.

Extension: Improve your model's accuracy with more/better data, or explore prompt design with an LLM and evaluate its bias and reliability.

Objectives: Students can train a simple machine-learning model, explain ML and LLM basics, and evaluate bias, transparency and ethics.

Assessment: Formative — Teachable Machine experiments and discussion. Summative — an AI research presentation + interactive AI build. (AO5 Citizenship & E-Safety · AO1 Programming)

Progression Across KS3

By the end of KS3, students will have:

  • Built strong digital literacy and productivity — becoming fluent with the Microsoft 365 apps (Word, Excel, PowerPoint, OneDrive, Teams, email) and tools such as Canva — managing files and accounts safely and responsibly. (AO2 Digital Literacy)
  • Developed computational thinking — algorithms, logical reasoning, abstraction, and decomposition — and applied it to unplugged, mechanical (Turing Tumble), low-level instruction-based (The School Computer), and programming tasks. (AO1 Programming)
  • Progressed from visual, block-based programming (Scratch: blocks → procedures → simulations) toward text-based programming, with a first taste of Python introduced through the Micro:bit. (AO1 Programming)
  • Built a sustained understanding of physical computing with the BBC Micro:bit — from LEDs and buttons (Y7), to sensors and data (Y8), to radio/IoT and MicroPython (Y9). (AO1 Programming)
  • Understood how computers, networks and data work — number systems, logic, hardware/software, the CPU and the fetch–execute cycle, how the internet works, and how data is represented (text, images, sound) — and collected, analysed, and presented data responsibly. (AO3 Systems & Data)
  • Explored the fundamentals of Artificial Intelligence — from AI foundations, to AI literacy, to hands-on machine learning — evaluating its ethics and impact on society. (AO5 Citizenship & E-Safety · AO3 Systems & Data)
  • Applied creativity, design, and independence to original digital projects — including creative coding and collaboration in Minecraft Education — researching, presenting, and working towards Coolest Projects. (AO4 Creativity & Projects)
  • Consistently applied e-safety and digital ethics, and reflected on their progress against the Emerging → Developing → Secure → Mastery rubric. (AO5 Citizenship & E-Safety)

By the time students reach KS4, every core GCSE strand has already been met at least once in a concrete, hands-on form.

Each stage of the KS3 curriculum builds progressively on the previous one, ensuring a clear and coherent development of knowledge, skills, and understanding.
