✨ What changed in v4

  • The curriculum now runs one book module per half-term across all three years, following Progress in Computing.
  • Coolest Projects is now an after-school club, not the Year 9 capstone.
  • Year 9 is book-based (Raspberry Pi, Algorithms, Python, Experience AI) and pairs each module with a short systems & theory topic to bridge into Year 10 GCSE Computer Science (Pearson Edexcel).
  • AI is delivered through Experience AI, and each year also has an extension module (Ext).

🗂️ Microsoft 365 routine

Every year opens HT1 with a short induction — Computer lab & Microsoft 365 (accounts, Teams, OneDrive) — so students can log in, collaborate and keep their work organised from day one, before the first module begins.

Assessment principle: short quizzes during units, and at least one assessed piece of evidence every half-term. All reported Emerging → Developing → Secure → Notable → Mastery.

Types of assessment — what each piece of evidence is, named in each year's units:

  • Quiz — a short knowledge check during a unit.
  • Written test — an end-of-unit test of understanding.
  • Presentation — present work to the class (Canva or PowerPoint).
  • Coding project — a working program built in Scratch, MakeCode or Python.
  • Practical project — a created product (graphics, video, website, data analysis or a physical-computing build).

Each assessment type has its own rubric — 3–4 criteria, each judged across five levels. Find the assessment's type in the year units and use the matching rubric below.

HT1: Induction + M16 — The ethics of computing

Focus: Induction: Computer lab & Microsoft 365 (accounts, Teams, OneDrive). Then M16: sourcing content responsibly; using technology responsibly; technology & the environment; technology & the law; moral dilemmas.

Assessment: Presentation — "Living in a digital world: pros and cons" (Canva or PowerPoint). The Microsoft 365 induction is a formative check.

HT2: M10 — Internet safety, cyber security & encryption

Focus: Digital footprint; passwords & phishing; malware; encryption; automating encryption; keeping yourself safe online.

Assessment: Written test — cyber-security & e-safety (including an encryption/decryption task).

HT3: M5 — Computing components

Focus: Computer hardware; measuring performance; peripherals; storage devices & media; the Internet of Things.

Assessment: Written test — computer components & systems.

HT4: M11 — Binary & computer logic

Focus: Logic gates; introducing binary; representing text; representing images.

Assessment: Practical project — represent text & images in binary (e.g. a pixel-art or binary-image task), plus a short logic check.

HT5: Turing Tumble — a marble-powered mechanical computer

Focus: A physical device (and free simulator) that makes sequence, logic and binary visible: build paths to route marbles, count in binary, release colour patterns, and build logic (AND/OR) with ramps, bits and interceptors. 🔗 Turing Tumble · Simulator

Assessment: Practical project — a Turing Tumble build challenge (e.g. a binary counter) with a short explanation.

HT6: BBC Micro:bit — physical computing

Focus: Program a physical BBC Micro:bit in block-based MakeCode, connecting code to the real world (inputs → process → outputs).

Assessment: Coding project — a Micro:bit build (e.g. dice, step counter, reaction-timer game).

Ext: M4 — Programming in Scratch

Focus: Introduction to the Scratch environment; sequencing; variables; selection; selection & logical operators; iteration.

Assessment (optional): Coding project — an interactive Scratch game or animation, for early finishers or spare time.

HT1: Induction + M1 — Getting started

Focus: Induction: Computer lab & Microsoft 365. Then M1: the internet & digital wellbeing; vector graphics; bitmap graphics; impossible photographs.

Assessment: Practical project — a vector/bitmap "impossible photograph" composite. The Microsoft 365 induction is a formative check.

HT2: M13 — Designing websites

Focus: Basic styling with CSS; images & lists; hyperlinks & navigation; layout & web-page design; designing a web page.

Assessment: Practical project — a website that is a learning tool for another subject, plus a written evaluation.

HT3: M12 — Sound & video editing

Focus: Sound editing & Audacity; audio effects; planning a video advert; creating a soundtrack; video editing & OpenShot; visual effects.

Assessment: Practical project — a finished video advert, plus a written evaluation.

HT4: M2 — Introducing spreadsheets

Focus: Formulae, replication & referencing; SUM/AVERAGE/MAX/MIN; Boolean operators, IF & COUNT; formatting, graphs & charts; modelling; theme-park challenges.

Assessment: Practical project (data) — a spreadsheet model or theme-park challenge with charts and findings.

HT5: M14 — Networking & the Internet

Focus: IP addressing & switches; domain names & DNS; packets & packet switching; the internet; connecting to the internet; a community guide to the internet.

Assessment: Written test — networks & the internet.

HT6: Experience AI (Raspberry Pi Foundation)

Focus: Lessons on artificial intelligence & machine learning — how ML learns from data, its applications, and AI ethics. 🔗 Experience AI

Assessment: Presentation — an AI & machine-learning explainer (how it works, plus an ethical issue).

Ext: M7 — Advanced spreadsheets

Focus: Drop-down lists, VLOOKUP & sorting; check boxes; macros; logical operators & the REPT function; making a self-marking quiz.

Assessment (optional): Practical project — a self-marking quiz built with macros, for early finishers or spare time.

Year 9 is a bridge to GCSE Computer Science (Pearson Edexcel, Year 10). Alongside the programming and algorithms modules, each half-term adds a paired systems & theory topic, so the two strands mirror the GCSE's own programming-plus-theory structure. Each half-term is assessed on both strands: a coding or practical piece for the programming, plus a short quiz or written test on the systems & theory topic (GCSE-style). Light-touch by default (a few lessons per half-term) — push harder where time allows.

HT1: Induction + The Raspberry Pi + Hardware & the CPU

Focus: Induction: Computer lab & Microsoft 365. Then getting started with the Raspberry Pi — the desktop & OS, files, connecting peripherals, first hands-on use (getting-started guide).

Systems & theory: components, RAM/ROM and the fetch–execute cycle (supported by The School Computer Simulator).

Assessment: Practical project — set up and use a Raspberry Pi (guided getting-started tasks). Quiz — hardware, RAM/ROM & the fetch–execute cycle. The Microsoft 365 induction is a formative check.

HT2: M8 — Algorithms + Data representation 1

Focus: Computational thinking to solve problems; pattern recognition; flow diagrams; Cholera in Soho; Malaria in Kitanga; Meet the Potato Heads.

Systems & theory: binary ↔ denary, binary addition & overflow, hexadecimal (deepens Year 7 binary).

Assessment: Written test — computational thinking & algorithms (including a flow-diagram task) and data representation (binary, binary addition & overflow, hexadecimal).

HT3: M6 — Python: sequence + Data representation 2

Focus: Computer programs; getting data from the user; data types; placeholders & lists; working with lists; working with strings.

Systems & theory: ASCII / character sets, bitmaps, sound & compression.

Assessment: Coding project — a Python program using sequence (input, data types, lists, strings). Quiz — data representation (ASCII, bitmaps, sound, compression).

HT4: M9 — Python: selection + Storage & the OS

Focus: Selection; decisions based on calculations; if/else; comparing strings & numbers; elif; multiple elifs.

Systems & theory: primary vs secondary storage and media, plus file management.

Assessment: Coding project — extend the program with selection (if / else / elif). Quiz — storage & the operating system.

HT5: M15 — Python: iteration + Networks

Focus: Repeating instructions; user-defined for loops; for loops with strings & lists; searching with for loops; while loops.

Systems & theory: LAN/WAN, IP, protocols, TCP/IP & packet switching (deepens Year 8 networking).

Assessment: Coding project — extend the program with iteration (loops). Written test — networks (LAN/WAN, protocols, TCP/IP, packet switching).

HT6: Experience AI + Issues & impact

Focus: Deeper artificial intelligence & machine learning — models, large language models and AI ethics (builds on Year 8). 🔗 Experience AI

Systems & theory: privacy, data-protection law & cyber security (leads on from Experience AI toward GCSE Topic 5).

Assessment: Presentation — an AI & machine-learning explainer. Written test — issues & impact (privacy, data-protection law, cyber security).

Ext: The School Computer Simulator

Focus: A model of a computer as a school building — office = CPU, instructions in the Primary Rooms (primary memory), data in the secondary rooms (secondary storage). Write & run simple low-level programs in the spreadsheet, and run it unplugged to act out the fetch–execute cycle. File: TheSchoolComputer.xlsx.

Assessment (optional): Coding project — write, run & explain a low-level program, for early finishers or spare time.

🚀 After-school enrichment — Coolest Projects club

Students who want to design and build a real project for the global Coolest Projects online showcase do so through the after-school club, which follows the Raspberry Pi Foundation's four-stage process:

  • 💡 Generate Ideas
  • 🛠️ Design & Create
  • 🐞 Test & Debug
  • 🎤 Present & Register

The full club plan and mentor resources are in the Coolest Projects club section below (also in CoolestProjects-Club-Plan.md).

Mentor plan · KS3 (open to all abilities) · runs across the year to the registration deadline (~May) · 2026–2027

What it isA weekly after-school club where students design, build and enter a real project in the global Coolest Projects online showcase. It sits alongside — not inside — the taught KS3 curriculum; the club is where students who want to go further make something of their own.
The outcomeBy the deadline, each student (solo or in a small team) has a finished project registered and submitted to Coolest Projects, with a short video or shared Scratch project and their answers to the three registration questions.
How it's runMentor-supported and project-led, not graded. Students work at their own pace through the Raspberry Pi Foundation's four-stage process, with the mentor coaching rather than teaching from the front, using the Mentor Plan and Youth Workbook.
Who it's forAny KS3 student, any experience level, working solo or in a small team. Eligibility: entrants are 18 or under; those under 13 need an adult to register on their behalf. Projects and videos are shared publicly with comments disabled, so parental consent and uploads are arranged early.

The four-stage journey — the spine of the club

The club moves through the four stages across the year, looping back to test and improve as real makers do. Download the current Youth Workbook, Stage 1–4 session plans and Mentor Plan from the guidance page — always the latest versions.

StageWhat students do
1 · Generate IdeasDecide who it's for and why; brainstorm (three ideas, "think big"); pick one; define what it will and won't do; choose the technology and category.
2 · Design & CreatePlan the user journey; sketch the screens or build; build a first working prototype — "just enough that someone can try it."
3 · Test & DebugSomeone else tries the prototype; note what works and what to fix; iterate, then decide the next action.
4 · Present & RegisterAnswer the three registration questions; finalise a Scratch description or record a video showing the project and the code; run the checklist; register and submit.

The three registration questions

  1. Tell us about your project. Why did you choose to make it?
  2. What did you find difficult while making it, and how did you work it out?
  3. Is there anything you would do differently, or add, if you had more time?

🧪 Optional skills tasters — on-ramps for beginners

Newcomers who don't yet know what to make can try one or two quick, hands-on tasters early on, then commit to a technology for their own project. Confident students can skip them and go straight to their own project.

  • Scratch / Games — a small playable game (builds on the Year 7 Scratch extension). Simplest registration: shared on the Scratch website, no video needed.
  • Web / AI "vibe coding" — a web page or simple educational simulator built with AI-assisted tools (e.g. Canva AI), learning to read, test and explain the code.
  • Micro:bit / Hardware — a physical input → process → output build in MakeCode (builds on Year 7 Micro:bit).

📝 Registration & showcase

  • Seven categories: Scratch · Games · Web · Mobile Apps · Hardware · Advanced Programming · Artificial Intelligence (newest). Students pick the one that fits, plus up to three topics.
  • Registration route: Scratch projects are shared on the Scratch website (no video). All other categories need a short video showing the project and the code, uploaded to YouTube or Vimeo (comments off, link public).
  • Checklist before submitting: one project per entrant; presented in English; video shows the code (non-Scratch); comments disabled; link public and viewable by anyone.
  • Deadline: register and submit by ~May (targeting Coolest Projects Online 2027) — confirm the exact date on the website.

🛡️ Safeguarding & logistics

  • Parental consent for public sharing and for uploading video to YouTube or Vimeo — arrange early. For under-13s, a parent or guardian uploads and registers on the student's behalf.
  • Public sharing with comments disabled; links not private.
  • Accounts & kit: Scratch and any AI-tool accounts set up in advance; Micro:bit kit and Raspberry Pi devices where used; work saved to OneDrive so nothing is lost across a long project.
  • AI-tools rule: the AI is the assistant, the student is the author — they must understand and be able to explain their own code (the video must show it).
